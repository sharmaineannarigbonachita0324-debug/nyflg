Divorce in New York

Your Guide to Getting Divorced in New York

Strict residency requirements and legal grounds apply before a New York court will grant a divorce. Other than the residency requirements, there is no waiting period. Explore the key legal questions below.

What Are the Residency Requirements for Divorce in NYC?

One of the following must exist before the courts grant you a divorce:

  • The marriage occurred in New York, and one spouse has lived here for a minimum of one year.
  • You lived in New York as a married couple, and one spouse has lived here for a minimum of one year.
  • The grounds stated for your divorce happened here, and one spouse has lived here for a minimum of one year.
  • You or your spouse has lived in New York for a minimum of two years.

Other than these residency requirements, there is no waiting period to get a divorce in New York.

Fault and Grounds for Divorce

So much of the emotional divorce process is about blame and fault. In New York, fault also has a legal impact: the person filing for divorce must provide grounds for that decision. There are seven accepted grounds for divorce:

  • There has been an irretrievable breakdown in the marriage for at least six months.
  • There has been cruel and inhuman treatment by one of the spouses within the previous five years.
  • One spouse has abandoned the other for over a year.
  • One spouse has been imprisoned for three years or more. This ground can also be used for up to five years after that spouse's release.
  • There is evidence of adultery during the time of the marriage.
  • The spouses have been legally separated for a year and have lived separately.
  • There was a Judgment of Separation created by the Supreme Court. This is a rarely used ground.

Dealing With the Legal Aspects

Because marriage is a legal and emotional relationship, many legal issues must be settled before a New York divorce is granted. Sometimes a couple can agree on these themselves and seek an uncontested divorce. Otherwise, the court decides. Issues to settle include:

  • Division of property
  • Spousal support
  • Child support
  • Child custody

When a couple can cooperate and negotiate terms with the help of a New York lawyer or mediator, it can save significant costs and time.

Contested Divorce in NY

There are many reasons couples end up in a contested divorce in NY. One spouse may not want the divorce or may feel disadvantaged, the spouses may not agree on decisions that must be made, or one spouse may be concerned about the other's truthfulness and transparency.

When divorcing spouses cannot reach the decisions or compromises necessary, they must rely on the legal system and litigation. Once the court makes its final decisions, both spouses are bound by them, whether they like them or not.

If you and your spouse cannot find common ground, skilled legal guidance is essential. No-Fault Divorce in NY

For couples who want to avoid the costly and time-consuming process of a contested divorce, New York allows an uncontested, or "no-fault," divorce. Although no-fault divorce has only been recognized in New York since 2010, it is now the most used ground for divorce in the state.

To pursue a no-fault divorce, you and your spouse must meet the residency requirements and agree:

  • That both of you want the divorce
  • That you are divorcing due to an irretrievable breakdown in your marriage
  • On the division of your marital property
  • On child custody arrangements and a parenting time schedule
  • On child support and spousal support

These issues can be mediated or negotiated with the help of a New York divorce lawyer, so that you make your own decisions instead of the court. Learn about uncontested divorce.

How Divorce Affects Your Children

One of the most significant decisions in a divorce is how you will provide for and co-parent your children. Who will be the primary custodial parent? How will you make decisions about their healthcare and education? Who will pay child support, and how much?

The New York family court system always focuses on the child's best interests. If parents cannot agree on how to co-parent, emotionally and financially, the court will decide for them. While the court considers each parent's wishes, it will always put the child's best interests ahead of the parents' convenience.

Ethan Steward and Martin Mohr can help you understand the full legal impact of every decision you make regarding your children, so you stay in control. Learn about child custody and visitation.

Division of Marital Property and Debt in NY

New York is an equitable distribution state. Separate property and debt stay with each spouse, while marital property and debt acquired during the marriage are divided equitably. Courts consider many factors, including:

  • The income of each spouse
  • The separate property of each spouse
  • The length of the marriage
  • Whether spousal support is a factor
  • The age and health of each spouse
  • Each spouse's education and earning capacity
  • Each spouse's wishes about particular belongings
  • How liquid the assets are

Equitable does not mean an even split. Some assets, such as the family home or a family business, cannot be split evenly, and an asset valuation is often needed. Couples who negotiate reasonably can save on costly appraisal fees and reduce points of contention.

More Family Law FAQs

What is a no-fault divorce in New York?

+

A no-fault divorce is based on the ground of "irretrievable breakdown," meaning the relationship has broken down for at least 6 months. To use this ground, the spouses must also have settled all economic issues, including debt, division of marital property, child support and custody, and spousal maintenance.

What are the requirements for getting a divorce in New York?

+

You must meet New York's residency requirement and at least one of the seven legal grounds for divorce. You meet the residency requirement if:

  • You or your spouse has lived in New York continuously for at least 2 years before the divorce
  • You or your spouse has lived in New York for at least 1 year and (1) you married in New York, (2) you lived in New York as a married couple, or (3) the grounds for your divorce happened in New York
  • Both of you are New York residents on the day the divorce starts, and the grounds for divorce happened in New York

What are the grounds for divorce in New York?

+

New York recognizes seven grounds for divorce:

  1. Irretrievable breakdown ("no fault") for at least 6 months
  2. Cruel and inhuman treatment, such as abuse or domestic violence, within the past 5 years
  3. Abandonment or "constructive" abandonment for 1 year or more
  4. Imprisonment for 3 years or more, usable while the spouse is in prison or up to 5 years after release
  5. Adultery, with proof that it occurred during the marriage
  6. Living apart for 1 year under a signed separation agreement
  7. Living apart for 1 year after a judgment of separation from the Supreme Court, a rarely used ground

Will I need to go to court to get divorced?

+

Not always. Uncontested divorces are commonly settled out of court: both spouses agree on the terms, usually with the help of divorce lawyers, and the agreement is presented to the court for approval. If you and your spouse cannot agree on any part of the divorce, it becomes a contested divorce and must be settled in court.

How is property divided in a New York divorce?

+

New York divides marital property "equitably," which is not necessarily a 50/50 split. Courts consider factors such as each spouse's age, health, and earning potential. Marital property is anything acquired during the marriage, regardless of whose name it is in. Separate property, such as assets owned before the marriage, gifts, and inheritances, is not divided.

FAQ

Divorce in New York: Common Questions

Direct answers to the questions we hear most. Every case is different, so call either office to talk through yours.

How long do I have to live in New York to file for divorce?

+

You or your spouse must have lived in New York continuously for at least 2 years, or for at least 1 year if you married in New York, lived in New York as a married couple, or the grounds for your divorce happened in New York. Other than these residency requirements, there is no waiting period to get a divorce in New York.

What are the grounds for divorce in New York?

+

New York recognizes seven grounds: irretrievable breakdown ("no fault") for at least 6 months; cruel and inhuman treatment within the past 5 years; abandonment for 1 year or more; imprisonment for 3 years or more; adultery; living apart for 1 year under a separation agreement; and living apart for 1 year after a judgment of separation.

What is the difference between a contested and an uncontested divorce?

+

In an uncontested divorce, both spouses agree on all key issues, including property, custody, and support, and the agreement is presented to the court. In a contested divorce, the spouses cannot agree, so the court decides those issues through litigation, and both spouses are bound by the result.

Can a divorce be resolved through mediation?

+

Yes. Many divorces are resolved through mediation or negotiation instead of a trial. Our attorneys work to minimize conflict and reach amicable agreements whenever feasible, and we vigorously advocate for your rights in court when necessary.

How is property divided in a New York divorce?

+

New York is an equitable distribution state. Marital property acquired during the marriage is divided fairly, which is not always 50/50, based on factors such as each spouse's income, age, health, and the length of the marriage. Separate property, such as assets owned before the marriage, gifts, and inheritances, is not divided.

Key Takeaways

  • A New York court usually needs to be the child's "home state" court before it can make a custody order.
  • Custody cases start with a petition in Family Court, or in Supreme Court when custody is part of a divorce.
  • The other parent must be properly notified, and the court decides based on the child's best interests.

Starting a custody case can feel overwhelming, especially when you are worried about your children and your relationship with them. Breaking the process into clear steps makes it easier to know what to expect and what to prepare.

Step 1: Make Sure New York Has Jurisdiction

Before a court can issue a valid custody order, it needs jurisdiction over your child's case. New York usually has jurisdiction when it is the child's "home state," which generally means the child has lived here for at least six consecutive months right before the case is filed.

Different rules can apply for very young children and in some other situations. Jurisdiction depends on where the child has lived, not simply where a parent lives today.

Step 2: File a Custody Petition in the Right Court

If you are only asking for custody or visitation, you usually file a petition in Family Court. If custody is part of a divorce, the Supreme Court decides it within the divorce case. In the Bronx, for example, Family Court and Supreme Court are separate courthouses.

Your petition must clearly state what you are asking the court to order and follow the court's procedural rules.

Step 3: Give Proper Notice to the Other Parent

The other parent, and in some cases other people who have physical custody of the child, must be properly notified and given a chance to be heard. Mistakes with notice are a common cause of delays.

Step 4: Understand the Best-Interests Standard

New York courts decide custody based on the child's best interests. Factors the court may weigh include:

  • The stability of each parent's home environment
  • Each parent's ability to provide guidance and emotional support
  • Each parent's fitness and financial circumstances
  • Each parent's willingness to support the child's relationship with the other parent

Domestic violence allegations matter

The court must consider the effect of domestic violence on the child, including the child's safety and emotional well-being. If you need protection, learn about orders of protection.

Step 5: Complete the Required Forms and Documents

You may need additional forms and supporting documents, which are available from the court clerk's office. In contested cases, especially when child support is also at issue, financial disclosure can become important.

Step 6: Prepare for Conferences, Hearings, or Trial

Most cases begin with a preliminary conference. If the parents cannot agree, the case may move to hearings or a trial, where evidence can include testimony, documents, and school and medical records. High-conflict cases usually take longer.

Parent education programs

In some custody and visitation cases, the court may order parents to complete a parent education program. It is not required in every case, and may not be ordered where there is a history of domestic violence.

Forensic evaluations

In heavily contested cases, the court may appoint a licensed forensic evaluator to assess the parents and the child. Learn what a forensic evaluator means for your custody case.

The Court Will Issue a Custody Order

The final order sets out legal custody, physical custody, and parenting time. If circumstances change later, you can ask the court to change the custody order. Fathers seeking to protect their role can learn more about fathers' rights in New York.

Why Filing Correctly Matters

Who is the better parent matters, but so do jurisdiction, the right court, proper notice, and procedural compliance. Getting those right from the start helps you avoid delays and keeps the focus on your child.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long must my child live in New York before I can file for custody here?

+

Generally, New York must be the child's home state, which usually means the child has lived in New York for at least six consecutive months right before you file. Different rules can apply for very young children and in some other situations.

Do I file for custody in Family Court or Supreme Court?

+

If you are only asking for custody or visitation, you usually file a petition in Family Court. If custody is part of a divorce, it is decided by the Supreme Court as part of the divorce case.

Will the court order a forensic evaluation in my custody case?

+

Not always. In heavily contested cases, the court may appoint a licensed forensic evaluator to assess the parents and the child and report to the court.
